# IMO 2026 Problem 1 — Complete Rigorous Proof

## Problem Statement

There are **2026** integers greater than 1 written on a blackboard, not necessarily distinct. In a move, Confucius chooses two integers $m > 1$ and $n > 1$ from different places on the blackboard and replaces them with
$$
\gcd(m,n) \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}.
$$
He continues to make moves while it is possible to do so.

**Prove that:**
1. The process always terminates after finitely many moves with exactly one integer $M > 1$ on the blackboard.
2. The value of $M$ does not depend on the choices Confucius makes.

---

## Notation and Preliminaries

For a positive integer $x$ and a prime $p$, let $v_p(x)$ denote the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorisation of $x$ (with $v_p(1) = 0$ for all $p$).
Define the **total prime factor count** (with multiplicity) of $x$ as
$$
\Omega(x) = \sum_{p\text{ prime}} v_p(x).
$$
For $x = 1$ we set $\Omega(1) = 0$.

For a board state $S$ (a multiset of 2026 positive integers), define two statistics:
- $K(S)$ = number of integers on the board that are strictly greater than $1$.
- $\Omega(S) = \sum_{x \in S} \Omega(x)$ = total number of prime factors (with multiplicity) across all numbers on the board.

The move replaces two numbers $m, n > 1$ with
$$
g = \gcd(m,n), \qquad L = \frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{g}.
$$
Using $\gcd(m,n) \cdot \operatorname{lcm}(m,n) = mn$, we have $L = mn/g^2$.
For a fixed prime $p$, write $a = v_p(m)$, $b = v_p(n)$ and assume without loss of generality $a \le b$. Then
$$
v_p(g) = \min(a,b) = a, \qquad
v_p(L) = v_p(mn/g^2) = a + b - 2a = b - a = |a - b|.
$$
Thus **for every prime $p$, the move replaces the pair of exponents $\{a, b\}$ with $\{a, b-a\}$** (assuming $a \le b$). This is exactly one step of the Euclidean algorithm on the pair $(a,b)$.

---

## Part 1: Termination and Exactly One Integer $> 1$ Remains

### Lemma 1 (Lexicographic Monovariant)

Define the **state measure** of a board $S$ as the pair
$$
\Phi(S) = \bigl( K(S),\; \Omega(S) \bigr)
$$
ordered lexicographically: $(K_1, S_1) < (K_2, S_2)$ iff $K_1 < K_2$, or $K_1 = K_2$ and $S_1 < S_2$.

**Every legal move strictly decreases $\Phi$.**

*Proof.* Consider a move on $m, n > 1$ with $g = \gcd(m,n)$, $L = mn/g^2$.

For each prime $p$, the sum of $p$-exponents changes from $a+b$ to $a + (b-a) = b = \max(a,b)$. The decrease in the sum of $p$-exponents is $\min(a,b) = v_p(g)$. Summing over all primes,
$$
\Delta \Omega = \Omega(\text{new}) - \Omega(\text{old}) = -\sum_p v_p(g) = -\Omega(g).
$$

We distinguish two cases based on $g$.

- **Case $g = 1$.** Then $\Omega(g) = 0$, so $\Omega(S)$ is unchanged. The new numbers are $g = 1$ and $L = mn > 1$. Exactly one of the two new numbers is $> 1$, whereas both $m, n$ were $> 1$. Hence $K$ decreases by $1$. Since $K$ decreases and $\Omega$ stays the same, $\Phi$ strictly decreases lexicographically.

- **Case $g > 1$.** Then $\Omega(g) \ge 1$, so $\Omega(S)$ decreases by at least $1$.  
  * If $m \neq n$, then $L = mn/g^2 = (m/g)(n/g) > 1$ (since $m/g, n/g \ge 1$ and at least one is $> 1$). Both new numbers are $> 1$, so $K$ is unchanged.  
  * If $m = n$, then $g = m = n$ and $L = 1$. Exactly one new number is $> 1$, so $K$ decreases by $1$.  

In all subcases, either $K$ decreases (with $\Omega$ possibly decreasing as well), or $K$ stays the same and $\Omega$ decreases by at least $1$. Hence $\Phi$ strictly decreases lexicographically. $\square$

### Lemma 2 (Termination)

The process terminates after finitely many moves.

*Proof.* Initially $K = 2026$ and $\Omega \ge 2026$ (each of the 2026 numbers is $> 1$, so $\Omega(x) \ge 1$). The measure $\Phi(S) = (K, \Omega)$ takes values in $\mathbb{N}^2$ with the well-founded lexicographic order. By Lemma 1, $\Phi$ strictly decreases at every move, so only finitely many moves are possible. $\square$

### Lemma 3 (Final State Has Exactly One Integer $> 1$)

When the process terminates, exactly one integer on the board is greater than $1$.

*Proof.* The process stops precisely when no move is possible, i.e. when fewer than two integers are $> 1$. Thus at termination $K \le 1$.

We show $K$ can never become $0$. Initially $K = 2026 \ge 1$. A move replaces two numbers $> 1$ with $(g, L)$. We check that at least one of $g, L$ is $> 1$:
- If $g = 1$, then $L = mn > 1$.
- If $g > 1$, then $g > 1$ by definition.

Thus $K$ never drops below $1$ (it either stays the same or decreases by $1$, but never jumps from $2$ to $0$). Since $K \ge 1$ always and $K \le 1$ at termination, we must have $K = 1$ at termination. $\square$

**Conclusion of Part 1.** The process terminates after finitely many moves, and the final board contains exactly one integer $M > 1$ (the other $2025$ integers are all $1$).

---

## Part 2: The Final Integer $M$ Is Independent of Choices

### Lemma 4 (Invariance of the $p$-adic Valuation GCD)

Fix a prime $p$. For a board state $S$, let
$$
G_p(S) = \gcd\bigl( \{ v_p(x) : x \in S \} \bigr)
$$
be the greatest common divisor of the multiset of $p$-adic valuations on the board.

**$G_p(S)$ is invariant under any legal move.**

*Proof.* A move picks two numbers $m, n$ and replaces them with $g = \gcd(m,n)$ and $L = mn/g^2$. For the prime $p$, their valuations $a = v_p(m)$, $b = v_p(n)$ are replaced by $\min(a,b)$ and $|a-b|$. Since
$$
\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(\min(a,b), |a-b|)
$$
(this is exactly one step of the Euclidean algorithm), the gcd of the two replaced valuations is preserved. All other numbers on the board are untouched, so the gcd of the entire multiset of $p$-valuations remains unchanged. $\square$

Let $G_p$ denote the invariant value computed from the **initial** 2026 integers.

### Lemma 5 (Final $p$-adic Valuation Equals the Invariant GCD)

Let $M$ be the unique integer $> 1$ on the board at termination. Then for every prime $p$,
$$
v_p(M) = G_p.
$$

*Proof.* At termination, the board consists of $M$ and $2025$ copies of $1$. For any prime $p$, the multiset of $p$-valuations is
$$
\{ v_p(M),\; \underbrace{0, 0, \dots, 0}_{2025\text{ times}} \}.
$$
The gcd of this multiset is $\gcd(v_p(M), 0, \dots, 0) = v_p(M)$.
By Lemma 4, this gcd equals the initial invariant $G_p$. Hence $v_p(M) = G_p$. $\square$

### Lemma 6 (Uniqueness of $M$)

The final integer is
$$
M = \prod_{p\text{ prime}} p^{G_p},
\qquad\text{where } G_p = \gcd\bigl( v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_{2026}) \bigr)
$$
and $a_1, \dots, a_{2026}$ are the initial integers.

*Proof.* By Lemma 5, every prime $p$ appears in $M$ with exponent exactly $G_p$. Since $M$ is uniquely determined by its prime factorisation, $M = \prod_p p^{G_p}$. This expression depends only on the initial multiset $\{a_1, \dots, a_{2026}\}$, not on the sequence of moves. $\square$

---

## Verification of Key Subtleties

1. **$M > 1$ is guaranteed.** Initially all 2026 integers are $> 1$, so at least one prime $p$ divides some initial number. For that prime, at least one initial $v_p(a_i) \ge 1$, hence $G_p \ge 1$. Thus $M \ge p^{G_p} \ge 2$.

2. **No conflict between primes.** The move operates on the same pair $(m,n)$ for all primes simultaneously. Lemma 4 shows the invariant holds for *each* prime independently, regardless of which pair is chosen. Lemma 3 guarantees global termination with $K=1$, which forces at most one positive $p$-valuation for *every* prime simultaneously. No additional “per-prime progress” argument is needed; the global termination proof (Lemma 2) already ensures the final state has the required form for all primes at once.

3. **The number $2026$ is arbitrary.** The proof works verbatim for any initial number $N \ge 2$ of integers $> 1$.

---

## Final Answer

The process always terminates after finitely many moves with exactly one integer $M > 1$ on the blackboard, where
$$
\boxed{M = \prod_{p\text{ prime}} p^{\,\gcd\bigl(v_p(a_1),\,v_p(a_2),\,\dots,\,v_p(a_{2026})\bigr)}}
$$
and $a_1, \dots, a_{2026}$ are the initial integers. This value depends **only** on the initial multiset, not on the choices Confucius makes during the process.
