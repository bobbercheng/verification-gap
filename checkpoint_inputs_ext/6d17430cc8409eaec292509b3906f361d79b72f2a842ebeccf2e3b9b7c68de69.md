# IMO 2026 Problem 1 – Complete Proof

**Problem.** There are 2026 integers greater than 1 written on a blackboard, not necessarily different. In a move, Confucius chooses two integers $m>1$ and $n>1$ from different places on the blackboard and replaces these two integers with $\gcd(m,n)$ and $\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}$. He continues to make moves while it is possible to do so.  
Determine the integer that remains on the board at the end, and prove that it does not depend on the choices made.

---

## 1.  Termination and the fact that exactly one integer $>1$ remains

Let the integers on the board be $x_1,\dots,x_{2026}$.  
In one move we replace $m,n>1$ by  
$$
g = \gcd(m,n), \qquad L = \frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}.
$$
Note that $g = \gcd(m,n) > 1$ because both numbers are $>1$.  
Also $L = \frac{mn}{\gcd(m,n)^2} > 1$ whenever $m \ne n$; if $m=n$ then $L=1$.

**Invariant 1 (strictly decreasing product).**  
The product of all numbers on the board strictly decreases.  
Indeed $m \cdot n = g \cdot \operatorname{lcm}(m,n) = g^2 \cdot L$, so $m n > L$ because $g>1$.  
The product is a positive integer, so it cannot decrease indefinitely.  
Hence the process terminates after finitely many moves.

**Invariant 2 (number of integers $>1$ never increases).**  
We replace two numbers $>1$ by $g>1$ and $L$.  
If $m=n$ then $L=1$, so the count of integers $>1$ drops by $1$.  
If $m\ne n$ then $L>1$, so the count stays the same.  
The count starts at $2026$ and never increases; it is always at least $1$ because $g>1$ is always produced.

The process stops exactly when fewer than two integers $>1$ remain.  
By Invariant 2 it can never drop below $1$, therefore the process terminates with **exactly one integer $M>1$** on the board.

---

## 2.  The final integer $M$ is independent of the choices made

We prove that $M$ is completely determined by the initial multiset of 2026 integers and does not depend on the sequence of moves.

### 2.1  Prime‑valuation invariant

Fix a prime $p$. For any integer $x>1$ on the board let $v_p(x)$ denote the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorisation of $x$.  
When we replace $m,n$ by $g=\gcd(m,n)$ and $L = \frac{mn}{g^2}$, the $p$-adic valuations transform as
$$
v_p(m),\; v_p(n) \;\longmapsto\; \min(v_p(m), v_p(n)),\; |v_p(m)-v_p(n)|.
$$
Indeed, $v_p(\gcd(m,n)) = \min(v_p(m),v_p(n))$ and $v_p(L) = v_p(m)+v_p(n)-2\min(v_p(m),v_p(n)) = |v_p(m)-v_p(n)|$.

**Lemma (Invariance of the $p$-adic gcd).**  
For any non‑negative integers $a,b$,
$$
\gcd(a,b) = \gcd\bigl(\min(a,b),\; |a-b|\bigr).
$$
*Proof.* $\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(\min(a,b), a) = \gcd(\min(a,b), a-\min(a,b)) = \gcd(\min(a,b), |a-b|)$. ∎

Therefore the **greatest common divisor of the multiset of $p$-adic valuations of all 2026 numbers on the board is invariant** throughout the process.  
Let $g_p$ denote this invariant value, computed from the initial 2026 integers.

### 2.2  Behaviour of $p$-adic valuations during the process

As long as at least two numbers on the board have positive $p$-adic valuation, they are both $>1$ (since they are divisible by $p$), so Confucius may choose that pair.  
Performing a move on such a pair replaces their $p$-valuations $a,b>0$ by $\min(a,b)$ and $|a-b|$.  
The sum of all $p$-adic valuations on the board **strictly decreases** in this case because
$$
a+b \;>\; \min(a,b) + |a-b| \quad\text{for } a,b>0.
$$
The sum of $p$-adic valuations is a non‑negative integer; therefore we cannot perform infinitely many moves that involve two positive $p$-valuations.  
Consequently, the process must eventually reach a state where **at most one number on the board has positive $p$-adic valuation**.

### 2.3  Determination of the final $p$-adic valuation

At termination there is exactly one integer $M>1$ on the board; all other 2025 integers equal $1$.  
For the prime $p$, all numbers equal $1$ have $v_p=0$. The number $M$ has some valuation $v_p(M) \ge 0$.  
The multiset of $p$-adic valuations at the end therefore consists of one entry $v_p(M)$ and 2025 zeros.  
The gcd of this multiset is exactly $v_p(M)$.

Because the gcd is invariant, we must have
$$
v_p(M) = g_p = \gcd\bigl(v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_{2026})\bigr),
$$
where $a_1,\dots,a_{2026}$ are the initial integers.

This argument holds for **every prime $p$ independently**. The operations on different primes are coupled only by the choice of which pair of numbers to pick; however, as argued above, as long as a prime $p$ has at least two positive valuations, those two numbers are $>1$ and form a legal pair. The process cannot “get stuck” for $p$ before its valuations reduce to the invariant gcd.

### 2.4  The final integer $M$

The final integer $M$ is uniquely determined by its $p$-adic valuations for all primes $p$:
$$
M = \prod_{p \text{ prime}} p^{\,g_p},
\qquad\text{where } g_p = \gcd\bigl(v_p(a_1), v_p(a_2), \dots, v_p(a_{2026})\bigr).
$$
This value depends only on the initial 2026 integers, not on the sequence of moves. ∎

---

## 3.  Answer

The integer that remains on the board at the end is
$$
\boxed{M = \prod_{p \text{ prime}} p^{\,\gcd(v_p(a_1),\,v_p(a_2),\,\dots,\,v_p(a_{2026}))}}
$$
where $v_p(x)$ denotes the exponent of the prime $p$ in the factorisation of $x$.  
This integer is completely determined by the initial multiset of 2026 integers and does not depend on the choices Confucius makes.
