## Problem
There are $2026$ integers greater than $1$ written on a blackboard, not necessarily different. In a move, Confucius chooses two integers $m>1$ and $n>1$ from different places on the blackboard and replaces these two integers with $\gcd(m,n)$ and $\frac{\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}$. He continues to make moves while it is possible to do so. (a) Prove that, regardless of the choices of Confucius, after finitely many moves, exactly one integer $M$ on the blackboard is greater than $1$. (b) Prove that the value of $M$ does not depend on the choices of Confucius.

## Approaches tried
- **Lexicographic descent:** Track the product of all entries and the number of entries greater than $1$. A move divides the product by $\gcd(m,n)$; if this gcd is $1$, the product is unchanged but the number of nonunit entries drops. This should prove termination.
- **Prime-adic exponent invariant:** For each prime $p$, the selected exponents $(a,b)$ become $(\min(a,b),|a-b|)$. The gcd of all $p$-adic exponents is invariant under this Euclidean-algorithm step, so it determines the exponent of $p$ in the final entry.

## Current best
The process terminates by the lexicographically decreasing pair consisting of the product of all blackboard entries and the number of entries greater than $1$. A move can never eliminate both selected nonunit entries, so a terminal board has exactly one nonunit. If the initial entries are $A_1,\ldots,A_{2026}$, the expected choice-independent final value is
\[
M=\prod_p p^{\gcd(v_p(A_1),\ldots,v_p(A_{2026}))},
\]
where the product is over the finitely many primes dividing the initial product.
