# IMO 2026 Problem 5 – Candidate Solution v2 (Revised)

**Problem.** Determine all functions $f : \mathbb{R}_{>0} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ such that
$$
\sqrt{\frac{x^2 + f(y)^2}{2}} \;\ge\; \frac{f(x) + y}{2} \;\ge\; \sqrt{x\,f(y)}
\qquad \forall x,y > 0.
$$

**Answer.** All solutions are $f(x) = x + c$ with $c \ge 0$.

---

## Proof

### 1.  A functional equation from the left inequality

Set $x = f(y)$ in the full chain. The left and right ends both become $f(y)$, so equality holds throughout:
$$
f(y) \;=\; \frac{f(f(y)) + y}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; f(f(y)) = 2f(y) - y \qquad (1)
$$
for all $y > 0$.

Define the *deviation* $d(x) = f(x) - x$. Then (1) becomes
$$
d(f(x)) = d(x) \qquad (2)
$$
and $f(x) = x + d(x) > 0$ for all $x > 0$.

---

### 2.  $d$ is non‑negative

Assume $d(a) < 0$ for some $a > 0$. By (2) the orbit $a_n = f^{\circ n}(a)$ satisfies
$a_{n+1} = a_n + d(a_n) = a_n + d(a)$, hence $a_n = a + n\,d(a)$.  
Since $d(a) < 0$, we eventually have $a_n \le 0$, contradicting $f : \mathbb{R}_{>0} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$.  
Thus
$$
d(x) \ge 0 \quad \forall x > 0. \qquad (3)
$$

---

### 3.  All positive values of $d$ are equal

Rewrite the right inequality using $f = x + d$:
$$
\frac{x + d(x) + y}{2} \ge \sqrt{x(y + d(y))}
\;\Longleftrightarrow\;
(x + d(x) + y)^2 \ge 4x(y + d(y)).
$$
Expanding and simplifying gives
$$
(x-y)^2 + d(x)^2 + 2x\,d(x) + 2y\,d(x) - 4x\,d(y) \ge 0 \qquad (4)
$$
for all $x,y > 0$.

Let $c,e > 0$ be two positive values taken by $d$. Choose $x_0, y_0 > 0$ with $d(x_0)=c$, $d(y_0)=e$.  
For integers $n,m \ge 0$, define the iterates along their orbits:
$$
x_n = f^{\circ n}(x_0) = x_0 + nc, \qquad
y_m = f^{\circ m}(y_0) = y_0 + me.
$$
(These are valid since $d$ is constant on each orbit by (2).)

Substitute $x = x_n$, $y = y_m$ into (4). Using $d(x_n)=c$, $d(y_m)=e$ and expanding:
$$
(x_n - y_m)^2 + c^2 + 2x_n c + 2y_m c - 4x_n e \ge 0.
$$
Substitute $x_n = x_0 + nc$, $y_m = y_0 + me$ and expand the quadratic part in $n,m$:
\begin{align*}
(x_n - y_m)^2 &= \bigl( (x_0 - y_0) + (c n - e m) \bigr)^2 \\
&= (c n - e m)^2 + 2(x_0 - y_0)(c n - e m) + (x_0 - y_0)^2.
\end{align*}
The full expression becomes
$$
(c n - e m)^2 \;+\; \underbrace{4c(c-e)n}_{\text{linear in }n} \;+\; O(n+m) \;\ge\; 0 \qquad (5)
$$
where the $O(n+m)$ term collects all terms linear in $n,m$ with constant coefficients (depending on $x_0,y_0,c,e$ but not on $n,m$).

**Claim.** If $c \ne e$, then (5) fails for some choice of non‑negative integers $n,m$.

*Proof of Claim.* We analyse the ratio $c/e$.

*Case 1: $c/e \in \mathbb{Q}$.* Write $c/e = p/q$ in lowest terms with $p,q \in \mathbb{N}$. Take $n_k = q k$, $m_k = p k$ for $k \in \mathbb{N}$. Then $c n_k - e m_k = 0$ for all $k$, so the quadratic term vanishes exactly. The linear term becomes $4c(c-e) q k$, which is negative for all sufficiently large $k$ precisely when $c < e$ (and positive when $c > e$). If $c > e$, we can swap the roles of $c$ and $e$ by symmetry (the inequality is symmetric in $x,y$ up to the roles of $c,e$ and the sign of the linear term), so either way we obtain a contradiction for large enough $k$.

*Case 2: $c/e \notin \mathbb{Q}$.* By **Dirichlet's approximation theorem** (or the theory of continued fractions), there exist infinitely many pairs of positive integers $(n_k, m_k)$ such that
$$
|c n_k - e m_k| < 1.
$$
For these pairs the quadratic term $(c n_k - e m_k)^2$ is bounded by $1$. The linear term is $4c(c-e)n_k + O(1)$. If $c > e$, then $c(c-e) > 0$, so the linear term grows like $+4c(c-e)n_k$. To get a contradiction we would need it to be negative; instead, if $c > e$ we can swap the roles of the two orbits (replace $x$ by $y$, which swaps $c$ and $e$ and changes the sign of the linear term). After swapping, the linear term becomes $4e(e-c)n_k$ with $e-c < 0$, i.e. negative and growing in magnitude like $4e(c-e)n_k$. In either case we obtain a sequence where the linear term is negative and its magnitude $\sim \Theta(n_k)$ while the quadratic term stays bounded. For sufficiently large $k$ the whole expression becomes negative, contradicting (5).

Since both cases lead to a contradiction when $c \ne e$, we must have $c = e$. Hence all positive values of $d$ coincide; there exists $c \ge 0$ such that
$$
d(x) \in \{0, c\} \quad \forall x > 0. \qquad (6)
$$

---

### 4.  $d$ cannot take both values $0$ and $c > 0$

Suppose $c > 0$ and there exist $x_0$ with $d(x_0)=c$ and $y_0$ with $d(y_0)=0$.  
Apply the **left** inequality with $x = x_0$, $y = y_0$:
$$
\sqrt{\frac{x_0^2 + y_0^2}{2}} \ge \frac{x_0 + c + y_0}{2}.
$$
Squaring and rearranging gives
$$
(x_0 - y_0)^2 \ge c^2 + 2c(x_0 + y_0). \qquad (7)
$$

Let $A = \{ x > 0 : d(x) = c \}$ and $B = \{ y > 0 : d(y) = 0 \}$. Both are non‑empty by assumption, and $A \cup B = \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ by (6).

**Lemma.** $\inf\{ |a - b| : a \in A,\; b \in B \} = 0$.

*Proof.* Suppose for contradiction that the distance between $A$ and $B$ is $\delta > 0$. Then the open $\delta/2$-neighbourhoods $U = \bigcup_{a \in A} (a - \delta/2, a + \delta/2)$ and $V = \bigcup_{b \in B} (b - \delta/2, b + \delta/2)$ are disjoint open subsets of $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ whose union is $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ (since every point belongs to $A$ or $B$). This contradicts the connectedness of $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$. $\square$

By the Lemma, there exist sequences $a_n \in A$, $b_n \in B$ with $|a_n - b_n| \to 0$. Take $x_0 = a_n$, $y_0 = b_n$ in (7). The left side tends to $0$, while the right side tends to $c^2 + 4c \lim b_n$ (or $c^2 + 2c \cdot 0$ if a subsequence of $b_n$ tends to $0$, which is impossible because then $a_n \to 0$ as well and the right side $\to c^2 > 0$). In any case the right side remains $\ge c^2 > 0$, a contradiction.

Therefore $d$ cannot take both values; it is constant: $d(x) \equiv c$ for some $c \ge 0$.

---

### 5.  Verification

For $f(x) = x + c$ ($c \ge 0$) the chain becomes
$$
\sqrt{\frac{x^2 + (y+c)^2}{2}} \;\ge\; \frac{x + y + 2c}{2} \;\ge\; \sqrt{x(y+c)}.
$$
Both inequalities are equivalent to $(x - y - c)^2 \ge 0$, true for all $x,y > 0$, with equality exactly when $x = y + c$.

---

**Conclusion.** The complete set of solutions is $f(x) = x + c$ for any constant $c \ge 0$.
