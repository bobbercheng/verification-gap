# IMO 2026 Problem 1 – Complete Proof

## Problem Statement

There are 2026 integers greater than 1 written on a blackboard, not necessarily distinct. In a move, Confucius chooses two integers $m > 1$ and $n > 1$ from different places on the blackboard and replaces them with $\gcd(m,n)$ and $\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}$. He continues to make moves while it is possible to do so. Prove that after finitely many moves, exactly one integer $M > 1$ remains on the blackboard, and that $M$ is uniquely determined by the initial 2026 integers.

---

## Proof

### 1. Prime Valuation Perspective

For any prime $p$ and integer $n \ge 1$, let $v_p(n)$ denote the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorization of $n$ (with $v_p(1)=0$). For any two integers $m,n > 1$,

$$
\gcd(m,n) = \prod_p p^{\min(v_p(m), v_p(n))}, \qquad
\operatorname{lcm}(m,n) = \prod_p p^{\max(v_p(m), v_p(n))}.
$$

Therefore the operation replaces $m,n$ with

$$
\gcd(m,n) \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)} = \prod_p p^{|v_p(m)-v_p(n)|}.
$$

**At the level of $p$-adic valuations, the move acts on each prime independently as**

$$
(a,b) \;\longmapsto\; \bigl(\min(a,b),\; |a-b|\bigr), \qquad a,b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}.
$$

---

### 2. Invariant: The GCD of the $p$-adic Valuations

**Claim 1.** For each prime $p$, the greatest common divisor of the multiset of $p$-adic valuations of the 2026 numbers on the board is invariant under the move.

*Proof.* A move replaces two valuations $a,b$ with $\min(a,b)$ and $|a-b|$. Since

$$
\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(\min(a,b), |a-b|),
$$

the gcd of the whole multiset does not change. $\square$

Let $g_p = \gcd\bigl(v_p(a_1),\dots,v_p(a_{2026})\bigr)$ be this invariant, where $a_1,\dots,a_{2026}$ are the initial integers.

---

### 3. Termination of the Process

**Claim 2.** The process terminates after finitely many moves.

*Proof.* Consider the pair

$$
\Bigl( \sum_{i=1}^{2026} \sum_p v_p(a_i) \;,\; \#\{i \mid a_i > 1\} \Bigr)
$$

ordered lexicographically (first component primary).

- If $\gcd(m,n) > 1$, then for some prime $p$ we have $\min(v_p(m),v_p(n)) \ge 1$. The operation replaces $v_p(m)+v_p(n)$ with $\min(v_p(m),v_p(n)) + |v_p(m)-v_p(n)| = \max(v_p(m),v_p(n))$, which is **strictly smaller** than the sum $v_p(m)+v_p(n)$ when both are positive. For other primes the sum of valuations does not increase. Hence the first component strictly decreases.
- If $\gcd(m,n) = 1$, then $\min(v_p(m),v_p(n)) = 0$ for every $p$, so the sum of valuations does not change. However, one of the two numbers becomes $1$ (since $\gcd=1$ and $\operatorname{lcm}/\gcd = mn > 1$), so the second component (the count of numbers $>1$) decreases by $1$.

Both components are non‑negative integers, and the second is bounded above by $2026$. Hence the process must terminate after finitely many steps. $\square$

---

### 4. Structure of the Final Configuration

**Claim 3.** When no further move is possible, exactly one integer on the board is greater than $1$.

*Proof.* A move is possible iff there are at least two integers $>1$. Termination therefore means at most one integer $>1$ remains. Could all numbers be $1$? Initially all numbers are $>1$, so their product $P > 1$. A move with $\gcd(m,n)>1$ replaces $mn$ with $\gcd \cdot (\operatorname{lcm}/\gcd) = \frac{mn}{\gcd} < mn$, so the product strictly decreases. A move with $\gcd=1$ leaves the product unchanged ($1 \cdot mn = mn$). Hence the product never increases and stays $\ge 1$; it can never drop to $1$ because that would require a strict decrease from $>1$ to $1$, which is impossible since all numbers are integers. Thus the final product is $>1$, so at least one number $>1$ remains. $\square$

Let $M > 1$ be the unique integer greater than $1$ on the board at termination (all other 2025 numbers equal $1$).

---

### 5. The Final Integer $M$ is Uniquely Determined

At termination, for each prime $p$ the multiset of $p$-adic valuations is

$$
\{ v_p(M),\; \underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{2025\text{ times}} \}.
$$

The gcd of this multiset is exactly $v_p(M)$. By Claim 1 this equals the invariant $g_p$. Hence

$$
v_p(M) = g_p = \gcd\bigl(v_p(a_1),\dots,v_p(a_{2026})\bigr)
$$

for every prime $p$. Consequently

$$
M = \prod_{p} p^{\gcd(v_p(a_1),\dots,v_p(a_{2026}))},
$$

which depends **only** on the initial 2026 integers and not on the sequence of moves chosen.

---

### 6. Conclusion

The process always terminates after finitely many moves. At termination exactly one integer $M > 1$ remains, and $M$ is given by the explicit formula above, hence is uniquely determined by the initial configuration. $\square$

---

## Remarks

1. The proof works for any starting number $N \ge 2$ of integers $>1$; the number 2026 plays no special role.
2. The invariant $g_p$ is exactly the exponent of $p$ in the **gcd of all pairwise gcds** of the initial numbers, or equivalently the exponent of $p$ in the gcd of all $a_i$ taken with multiplicity of prime factors. Indeed, $g_p = \min_i v_p(a_i)$ when all $v_p(a_i)$ share a common divisor, but the gcd of valuations is the precise invariant.
3. The operation $(a,b) \mapsto (\min(a,b), |a-b|)$ on valuations is exactly the Euclidean algorithm step on the pair $(a,b)$; the invariant is the gcd of the whole multiset, which is preserved by the Euclidean algorithm applied to any pair.
