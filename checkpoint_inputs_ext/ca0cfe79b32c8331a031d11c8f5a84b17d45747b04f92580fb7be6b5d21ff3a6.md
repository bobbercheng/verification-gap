# imo-2026-01 — tracking file
## Status
solved
## Problem
There are $2026$ integers greater than $1$ written on a blackboard, not necessarily different. In a move, Confucius chooses two integers $m>1$ and $n>1$ from different places on the blackboard and replaces these two integers with $\gcd(m,n)$ and $\frac{\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}$. He continues to make moves while it is possible to do so. (a) Prove that, regardless of the choices of Confucius, after finitely many moves, exactly one integer $M$ on the blackboard is greater than $1$. (b) Prove that the value of $M$ does not depend on the choices of Confucius.

## Approaches tried
- prime factorization analysis: v_p(gcd)=min, v_p(lcm)=max, v_p(lcm/gcd)=|a-b|, operation is (a,b)->(min,|a-b|) — Euclidean subtraction.
- total product P = prod x_i : P_{new}=P_{old}/gcd(m,n) nonincreasing, integer.
- count k of entries >1 nonincreasing, decreases when gcd=1 or m=n, stays otherwise.
- termination: if infinite, k eventually constant >=2, then all remaining moves have gcd>1, so P strictly decreasing infinitely impossible.
- per prime gcd invariance: G_p = gcd_i v_p(x_i) preserved; final single survivor must have exponent G_p, hence M = product p^{G_p} independent.

## Current best
(a) k_t counting >1 never increases, can decrease at most N-1 times; after it stabilizes, product P strictly decreases on each move, so cannot be infinite. Termination forces k=1.
(b) For each prime p, G_p=gcd_i v_p(x_i) is invariant under (a,b)->(min,|a-b|). At final state with one >1 number M, G_p = v_p(M), so M = prod p^{gcd_i v_p(initial_i)} independent of choices.

## Full proof
We work with $N=2026$; proof works for any $N\ge 2$. Let the numbers be $x_1,\dots ,x_N$.

Prime factorization. For $m,n>1$ write $m=\prod p^{a_p}$, $n=\prod p^{b_p}$ with $a_p,b_p\ge0$,
only finitely many $>0$. Then
\[
v_p(\gcd(m,n))=\min(a_p,b_p),\quad v_p(\operatorname{lcm}(m,n))=\max(a_p,b_p).
\]
Hence
\[
v_p\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}\right)=\max(a_p,b_p)-\min(a_p,b_p)=|a_p-b_p|.
\tag{1}
\]
Consequently
\[
\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)=\frac{mn}{\gcd(m,n)},\qquad
\gcd(m,n)\cdot\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)=\frac{mn}{\gcd(m,n)}.
\tag{2}
\]
So the unordered pair $\{m,n\}$ is replaced by
$\{g,\ell/g\}$ where $g=\gcd(m,n)$, $\ell=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$.
Per prime the pair of exponents $(a_p,b_p)$ becomes
\[
(\min(a_p,b_p),\;|a_p-b_p|)
\tag{3}
\]
up to order. This is the subtractive Euclidean step.

Product. Let $P_t=\prod_{i=1}^N x^{(t)}_i$ after $t$ moves. If the $(t+1)$-st move uses $m,n$ with $g=\gcd(m,n)$,
\[
P_{t+1}=P_t\cdot\frac{g\cdot \ell/g}{mn}=P_t\cdot\frac{\ell}{mn}=P_t/g
\tag{4}
\]
by (2). Hence $P_{t+1}\le P_t$ integer, $P_{t+1}<P_t$ iff $g>1$, equality iff $g=1$.

At least one result $>1$. If $m>1,n>1$ then not both new numbers are $1$:
if $\gcd(m,n)=1$ then $\ell/g=mn>1$; if $\ell/g=1$ then $\ell=g$,
and $g\mid m\mid\ell$ gives $m=g$, similarly $n=g$, so $m=n=g>1$.
Thus in a move one or two of the replacements are $>1$.

Counting $>1$ entries. Let $k_t=|\{i:x^{(t)}_i>1\}|$.
Consider a move on $m\neq n$:

- $g=1$: $m,n$ coprime, $>1$, new pair is $\{1,mn\}$ with $mn>1$.
  So $k_{t+1}=k_t-1$.

- $1<g$: $g>1$. If $m=n$ then $\ell/g=1$, new pair $\{g,1\}$, $k_{t+1}=k_t-1$.
  If $m\neq n$ then $g=\ell$ would imply $m=n$ (above), so $\ell/g>1$.
  Both $g>1$ and $\ell/g>1$, hence $k_{t+1}=k_t$.

In all cases $k_{t+1}\le k_t$ and $k_{t+1}\ge k_t-1$. In particular $k_t$ is nonincreasing.

No jump to $0$. Since a move replaces two $>1$ numbers by at least one $>1$,
$k_t$ cannot go from $1$ to $0$; from $\ge2$ it drops by at most $1$.
Initially $k_0=N$. So $k_t\ge1$ as long as defined.

Finiteness. Suppose an infinite sequence of moves existed.
$k_t$ nonincreasing, integer, bounded below by $1$, so it can decrease only
finitely many times (at most $N-1$). Thus there is $T$ after which
$k_t=k$ is constant. If $k=1$ the process would have stopped (no two $>1$ entries),
contradicting infinitude. Hence $k\ge2$ for $t\ge T$.

For $t\ge T$, $k_{t+1}=k_t$, so every move after $T$ must be of the third type:
$m\neq n$, $g>1$, both replacements $>1$. Then (4) gives $P_{t+1}=P_t/g<P_t$,
strictly decreasing. Thus $(P_t)_{t\ge T}$ is a strictly decreasing infinite
sequence of positive integers, impossible. Hence no infinite sequence exists.
Every choice sequence terminates after finitely many moves.

Terminal state. A state is terminal iff $k_t\le1$, i.e., fewer than two entries
$>1$. Since $k_t\ge1$ argued, termination implies $k_t=1$: exactly one integer
$M>1$ remains, the other $N-1$ are $1$. This proves (a).

Invariants for (b). Fix a prime $p$. Let $a^{(t)}_{i}=v_p(x^{(t)}_i)\ge0$.
Let
\[
G^{(t)}_p=\gcd\bigl(a^{(t)}_1,\dots ,a^{(t)}_N\bigr)
\]
with convention $\gcd(0,\dots ,0)=0$ and $\gcd(0,\dots ,0,b,\dots)= \gcd$ of the
non-zero part. Claim $G^{(t)}_p$ is invariant.

Indeed (3): $a_i,a_j$ become $a'_i=\min(a_i,a_j)$, $a'_j=|a_i-a_j|$.
Now $\gcd(a_i,a_j)=\gcd(\min(a_i,a_j),|a_i-a_j|)$:
if $a_i\le a_j$, $\gcd(a_i,a_j)=\gcd(a_i,a_j-a_i)$; symmetric; also holds when
one is $0$ ($\gcd(0,b)=b$). For any finite set,
$\gcd$ of the set equals $\gcd(\gcd(a_i,a_j),\text{rest})$.
Hence
\[
\gcd(\text{all before})=\gcd\bigl(\gcd(a_i,a_j),\text{rest}\bigr)
= \gcd\bigl(\gcd(a'_i,a'_j),\text{rest}\bigr)
= \gcd(\text{all after}).
\]
So $G^{(t)}_p$ is preserved by each move.

Let initial $G_p=G^{(0)}_p$. Let final situation be $y_1,\dots ,y_N$ with
$y_{k}=M>1$, $y_i=1$ for $i\neq k$. Then for each $p$,
$a^{(\text{final})}_i=0$ for $i\neq k$ and $a^{(\text{final})}_k=v_p(M)$.
Hence
\[
G^{(\text{final})}_p=\gcd(0,\dots ,0,v_p(M),0,\dots ,0)=v_p(M)
\]
(0 if $v_p(M)=0$). By invariance $v_p(M)=G_p$.

Thus
\[
v_p(M)=\gcd_{1\le i\le N} v_p(x^{(0)}_i)\qquad\text{for every prime }p,
\]
and
\[
M=\prod_{p} p^{\,\gcd_i v_p(x^{(0)}_i)}.
\tag{5}
\]
The right side depends only on the initial multiset, not on choices.
In particular if $p$ never occurs, $G_p=0$. If some prime occurs,
its gcd is $\ge1$ because at least one exponent $>0$ and zeros do not affect
gcd beyond taking the gcd of the positive ones (e.g., $\gcd(1,0)=1$),
so the product is $>1$, consistent with (a).

Hence regardless of the sequence of choices, when the process stops the
unique remaining $>1$ integer equals the number in (5); its value does not
depend on choices. This proves (b).

Remark: For $N=2$ this gives $M=\prod p^{\gcd(v_p(A),v_p(B))}$, e.g.,
$12,18\to6$.
∎
