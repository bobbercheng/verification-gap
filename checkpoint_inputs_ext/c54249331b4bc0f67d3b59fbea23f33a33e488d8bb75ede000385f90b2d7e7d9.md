# imo-2026-01 — tracking file

## Status
solved

## Problem
There are $2026$ integers greater than $1$ written on a blackboard, not necessarily different. In a move, Confucius chooses two integers $m>1$ and $n>1$ from different places on the blackboard and replaces these two integers with $\gcd(m,n)$ and $\frac{\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}$. He continues to make moves while it is possible to do so.
(a) Prove that, regardless of the choices of Confucius, after finitely many moves, exactly one integer $M$ on the blackboard is greater than $1$.
(b) Prove that the value of $M$ does not depend on the choices of Confucius.

## Approaches tried
- Brute-force simulation for small cases to guess the final value $M$ and invariants.
- Discovered that for each prime $p$, the gcd of the exponents of $p$ across all numbers is invariant, and the final $M$ equals $\prod_p p^{\gcd(\text{exponents})}$.
- Termination proved via monovariant $K+\sum\Omega$ strictly decreasing (where $K$ counts numbers $>1$, $\Omega$ counts prime factors with multiplicity).
- Invariant proof uses the fact that on the exponent multiset the move acts as $(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto(\min(\alpha,\beta),|\alpha-\beta|)$, which preserves the gcd of the whole multiset.

## Current best
The final unique integer $M>1$ equals
\[
M = \prod_{p\text{ prime}} p^{\,d_p},\qquad
d_p = \gcd\bigl(v_p(a_1),v_p(a_2),\dots,v_p(a_{2026})\bigr).
\]
where $a_1,\dots,a_{2026}$ are the initial numbers and $v_p(a)$ is the exponent of $p$ in $a$. Both parts are proved below.

## Full proof
Let $N=2026$ and let the initial numbers be $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_N$ (all integers $>1$). At any moment we denote the numbers on the board by $x_1,\dots,x_N$ (some may become $1$ during the process).

---
### 1. Termination – proof of part (a)

For a positive integer $t$ let $\Omega(t)$ be the number of prime factors of $t$ counted with multiplicity; set $\Omega(1)=0$.
Define
\[
K = \#\{\,i \mid x_i > 1\,\},\qquad 
S = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Omega(x_i).
\]
Both $K$ and $S$ are non‑negative integers.

A move picks two distinct indices $i,j$ with $x_i,x_j>1$. Put $m=x_i$, $n=x_j$. Let
\[
g=\gcd(m,n),\qquad h=\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}=\frac{mn}{g^2}.
\]
The two numbers $m,n$ are replaced by $g$ and $h$. Write $m=g a$, $n=g b$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$; then $h=ab$.

We analyse how $K$ and $S$ change.

* **Case $g=1$.**  
  Then $h=mn>1$. The new pair is $(1,\,mn)$. Consequently
  \[
  K' = K-1,\qquad
  S' = S-\Omega(m)-\Omega(n)+\Omega(1)+\Omega(mn)=S.
  \]

* **Case $g>1$ and $m=n$.**  
  Then $a=b=1$, so $h=1$. The new pair is $(g,1)$. Hence
  \[
  K' = K-1,\qquad
  S' = S-2\Omega(m)+\Omega(g)+\Omega(1)=S-\Omega(m)\le S-1.
  \]

* **Case $g>1$ and $m\neq n$.**  
  Then $a,b$ are not both $1$, so $h=ab>1$. Both $g>1$ and $h>1$.
  \[
  K' = K,\qquad
  S' = S-\Omega(m)-\Omega(n)+\Omega(g)+\Omega(h).
  \]
  Since $\Omega(m)+\Omega(n)=2\Omega(g)+\Omega(a)+\Omega(b)$ and $\Omega(h)=\Omega(a)+\Omega(b)$,
  \[
  S' = S-\Omega(g)\le S-1.
  \]

In every case either $K$ decreases or $S$ decreases (or both). Therefore the quantity
\[
K+S
\]
strictly decreases with each move. Being a non‑negative integer, it cannot decrease forever; after finitely many moves the process must stop.

The process stops exactly when fewer than two numbers on the board exceed $1$, i.e. $K\le 1$. Could we ever obtain $K=0$ (all numbers equal to $1$)? If a move produced $K=0$, it would have to replace two numbers $>1$ by two $1$'s. But in the three cases above the new pair contains at most one $1$: in the first case one $1$ and one $>1$, in the second one $1$ and one $>1$, in the third no $1$. Hence every move leaves at least one number $>1$ behind; consequently once the board contains only one number $>1$, no further move is possible and exactly one integer $M>1$ remains. This proves part (a).

---
### 2. Invariance of the exponent–gcd – proof of part (b)

Fix an arbitrary prime $p$. For an integer $t\ge 1$ denote by $v_p(t)$ the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorisation of $t$ ($v_p(1)=0$).

In a move on numbers $m,n$ with $\alpha=v_p(m)$, $\beta=v_p(n)$,
\[
v_p(\gcd(m,n)) = \min(\alpha,\beta),\qquad
v_p\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}\right)
= \alpha+\beta-2\min(\alpha,\beta)=|\alpha-\beta|.
\]
Thus the multiset $\mathcal E_p$ of the $N$ values $v_p(x_1),\dots,v_p(x_N)$ undergoes the following elementary change:
two elements $\alpha,\beta$ are removed and replaced by $\min(\alpha,\beta)$ and $|\alpha-\beta|$.

Let $d$ be the greatest common divisor of all numbers in the current multiset $\mathcal E_p$ (if all elements are $0$ we set $d=0$; otherwise $d$ is the usual positive greatest common divisor).  
We claim that $d$ does **not** change during a move.

Indeed, let the old multiset have gcd $d$, and let the new multiset $\mathcal E'_p$ be obtained by replacing $\alpha,\beta$ with $\alpha'=\min(\alpha,\beta)$, $\beta'=|\alpha-\beta|$.
Because $d\mid\alpha$ and $d\mid\beta$, we have $d\mid\alpha'$ and $d\mid\beta'$; together with $d$ dividing every other element, $d\mid\operatorname{gcd}(\mathcal E'_p)$.
Conversely, let $d'=\operatorname{gcd}(\mathcal E'_p)$. Then $d'\mid\alpha'$ and $d'\mid\beta'$.
If $\alpha\le\beta$ then $\alpha=\alpha'$ and $\beta=\alpha'+\beta'$, so $d'\mid\alpha$ and $d'\mid\beta$; the case $\alpha>\beta$ is symmetric.
Thus $d'$ divides all elements of the old multiset, so $d'\mid d$.
Hence $d'=d$, proving the invariance.

Initially the multiset is $\{v_p(a_1),\dots,v_p(a_N)\}$; denote its gcd by $d_p$. Throughout the whole process the gcd of the current multiset $\mathcal E_p$ stays equal to $d_p$.

When the process ends we have exactly one number $M>1$ and $N-1$ ones. Therefore
\[
\mathcal E_p = \{\,v_p(M), \underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{N-1\text{ times}}\,\}.
\]
The gcd of this multiset is $v_p(M)$ (because $\gcd(x,0,\dots,0)=x$). By the invariant,
\[
v_p(M) = d_p \qquad\text{for every prime } p.
\]

Consequently
\[
M = \prod_{p\text{ prime}} p^{\,d_p},
\qquad\text{where}\qquad
d_p = \gcd\bigl(v_p(a_1),v_p(a_2),\dots,v_p(a_N)\bigr).
\]
The right‑hand side depends only on the original $N$ integers and not on the particular sequence of moves chosen by Confucius. This completes part (b).

---
*Remark.* The number $2026$ plays no special role; the same statements hold for any $N\ge 2$ initial integers $>1$.
