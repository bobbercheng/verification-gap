# imo-2026-01 — tracking file

## Status
solved

## Problem
There are $2026$ integers greater than $1$ written on a blackboard, not necessarily different. In a move, Confucius chooses two integers $m>1$ and $n>1$ from different places on the blackboard and replaces these two integers with $\gcd(m,n)$ and $\frac{\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}$. He continues to make moves while it is possible to do so. (a) Prove that, regardless of the choices of Confucius, after finitely many moves, exactly one integer $M$ on the blackboard is greater than $1$. (b) Prove that the value of $M$ does not depend on the choices of Confucius.

## Approaches tried
- Elementary potential on number of entries >1 and total product (for termination) — works.
- p-adic valuations: the gcd of the multiset of exponents for each prime is invariant under moves; this determines $M$ uniquely — works.
- (Supporting) direct simulation of many instances confirms path-independence of $M$ and the closed-form $M=\prod_p p^{g_p}$.

## Current best
Parts (a) and (b) are fully proved. The final $M$ equals $\prod_p p^{g_p}$ where $g_p$ is the gcd of the initial $p$-adic valuations of the $2026$ numbers (and this quantity is invariant). Termination follows from a lexicographic potential $(k,P)$ where $k$ is the number of entries $>1$ and $P$ is the product of all board entries.

## Full proof

We work throughout with a fixed multiset (or ordered tuple) of $N=2026$ positive integers on the blackboard; initially each of them is an integer strictly greater than $1$. For a pair of integers $m,n>1$ write
\[
g:=\gcd(m,n),\qquad \ell:=\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{\gcd(m,n)}.
\]
A legal move consists of choosing two board positions occupied by integers $m>1$ and $n>1$ and replacing those two entries by $g$ and $\ell$ (in either order). Note the elementary identities
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prod}
mn=\gcd(m,n)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\qquad\text{and}\qquad g\cdot\ell=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)=\frac{mn}{g}.
\end{equation}
In particular $g\ell=mn/g$, so the product of the two new numbers is $mn/g$. Since $m,n>1$ we also have $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)>1$, whence at least one of $g,\ell$ is greater than $1$.

### Preliminary facts on valuations

**Lemma 1** (effect of a move on valuations).  
Fix a prime $p$. Let $\alpha=v_p(m)$ and $\beta=v_p(n)$. Then
\[
v_p(g)=\min(\alpha,\beta),\qquad v_p(\ell)=\max(\alpha,\beta)-\min(\alpha,\beta).
\]
Consequently
\[
\gcd\bigl(v_p(g),v_p(\ell)\bigr)=\gcd(\alpha,\beta).
\]

*Proof of Lemma 1.*  
By the usual formulae for valuations of gcd and lcm we have $v_p(g)=\min(\alpha,\beta)$ and $v_p(\operatorname{lcm}(m,n))=\max(\alpha,\beta)$, so
\[
v_p(\ell)=\max(\alpha,\beta)-\min(\alpha,\beta).
\]
Assume without loss of generality that $\alpha\le\beta$. The new pair of valuations is then $(\alpha,\beta-\alpha)$, and
\[
\gcd(\alpha,\beta-\alpha)=\gcd(\alpha,\beta)
\]
by the elementary property of the gcd. The case $\beta\le\alpha$ is symmetric. $\square$

**Lemma 2** (invariance of the gcd of a multiset of exponents).  
Let $S$ be a finite multiset of non-negative integers. Suppose two elements $\alpha,\beta\in S$ are replaced by $\min(\alpha,\beta)$ and $|\alpha-\beta|$. Then the greatest common divisor of all elements of the multiset is unchanged.

*Proof of Lemma 2.*  
Let $d=\gcd(S)$. Then $d$ divides $\alpha$ and $\beta$, so $d$ divides both $\min(\alpha,\beta)$ and $|\alpha-\beta|$; thus $d$ still divides every element of the new multiset. Conversely, any common divisor of the new multiset divides both new numbers and therefore divides their sum (which equals $\max(\alpha,\beta)$) and their difference (which equals $|\alpha-\beta|$), hence divides both $\alpha$ and $\beta$. It therefore divides every element of the old multiset. $\square$

As an immediate corollary of Lemmas 1 and 2 we obtain:

**Corollary 3.**  
For every prime $p$ define
\[
g_p:=\gcd\bigl\{v_p(x):x\text{ is currently written on the blackboard}\bigr\},
\]
where the gcd of an empty set is taken to be $0$ and $\gcd(0,a)=a$. Then every legal move leaves the value of $g_p$ unchanged. In particular each $g_p$ equals its value computed from the initial board.

### Part (a): termination with exactly one entry greater than 1

Let $k$ denote the number of entries on the board that are strictly greater than $1$, and let $P$ denote the product of all $N$ entries currently on the board. Initially $k=N=2026\ge 2$ and $P$ is a positive integer. We claim that every legal move strictly decreases the pair $(k,P)$ in the lexicographic order on $\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}_{>0}$.

Indeed, consider a legal move performed on two entries $m,n>1$, replaced by $g$ and $\ell$. There are three exhaustive sub-cases according to how many of $g,\ell$ exceed $1$:

- Both $g>1$ and $\ell>1$. Then $k$ is unchanged. From \eqref{eq:prod} the new product equals the old product divided by $g$. Since $g=\gcd(m,n)\ge 1$ and both replacement numbers exceed $1$, we cannot have $g=1$ (if $g=1$ then $\ell=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)=mn>1$, but then the product of the new numbers would be $\ell=mn$ while the formula $g\ell=mn/g$ forces $g=1$ and the product is unchanged — wait: if $g=1$ then exactly one of $g,\ell$ exceeds 1, namely $\ell$). Thus in the present sub-case we necessarily have $g\ge 2$, so the new product $P'$ satisfies $P'=P/g\le P/2<P$. Hence $(k,P)$ strictly decreases.
- Exactly one of $g,\ell$ exceeds $1$. Then the new value of $k$ is the old value minus $1$ (two numbers $>1$ are removed and only one number $>1$ is inserted). The product may increase or decrease, but the first component of the potential already drops.
- Neither $g$ nor $\ell$ exceeds $1$. This is impossible: $g\cdot\ell=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\ge\max(m,n)>1$, so at least one of them is greater than $1$.

Consequently every move strictly decreases $(k,P)$ lexicographically. Since $k$ is a non-negative integer and $P$ is a positive integer, there can be only finitely many successive decreases. Therefore only finitely many moves are possible.

When no further move is possible we necessarily have $k\le 1$ (otherwise two entries $>1$ could still be chosen). It remains only to rule out $k=0$. But initially $k=N\ge 1$, and we claim that a move never decreases $k$ below $1$. More precisely: the only way $k$ can drop is the second sub-case above, in which exactly one of the two replacement numbers exceeds $1$; thus after that move we still have $k_{\mathrm{new}}=k_{\mathrm{old}}-1\ge 1$ whenever $k_{\mathrm{old}}\ge 2$. (Alternatively: by Corollary 3, if some prime $p$ divides at least one initial entry then $g_p\ge 0$; but if the board ever consisted entirely of $1$s we would have every valuation equal to $0$, so $g_p=0$. If moreover some initial valuation is positive then the final board of all $1$s would force $g_p=0$, which is consistent only if $g_p=0$ already initially; but even then the product identity shows $P$ is always a positive integer divisible by the initial product of the numbers after suitable powers, and more simply: from $g\cdot\ell=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)>1$ a move always leaves at least one entry $>1$ among the two it writes, so when two entries $>1$ are replaced the number of entries $>1$ drops by at most $1$ and cannot jump from $2$ directly to $0$.) Hence at termination we have exactly $k=1$, i.e., exactly one integer $M$ on the blackboard is greater than $1$, and all other entries are equal to $1$.

This proves part (a).

### Part (b): uniqueness of $M$

By part (a) every sequence of moves terminates after finitely many steps with a board consisting of a single integer $M>1$ and $N-1$ ones. For this terminal board and for every prime $p$ we have
\[
g_p=\gcd\bigl(v_p(M),v_p(1),\dots,v_p(1)\bigr)=\gcd\bigl(v_p(M),0,\dots,0\bigr)=v_p(M).
\]
By Corollary 3 the quantities $g_p$ are independent of the sequence of moves performed; they are completely determined by the initial board. Therefore the $p$-adic valuation of the terminal $M$ is independent of the sequence of moves, for every prime $p$. Since a positive integer is uniquely determined by its valuations, the value of $M$ itself is independent of the choices of Confucius.

(Explicitly, if $a_1,\dots,a_N$ are the initial numbers, then
\[
M=\prod_p p^{g_p},\qquad g_p=\gcd\bigl(v_p(a_1),\dots,v_p(a_N)\bigr),
\]
the product running over all primes; only finitely many exponents are positive.)

This proves part (b).

### Remarks
- The same argument works verbatim for any finite number $N\ge 1$ of initial integers greater than $1$ (if $N=1$ there are no moves and $M$ is already the unique entry).
- The hypothesis that every initial entry is greater than $1$ is used only to guarantee that moves are possible at the outset when $N\ge 2$; the proofs of invariance and of the form of $M$ never use that the initials exceed $1$, only that they are positive integers.
