# IMO 2026 Problem 6 – Rigorous Solution

## Problem Restatement

Let $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ be an infinite sequence of integers $> 1$ such that for every positive integer $n$,
$$a_{n+1} = \min\{ m \in \mathbb{Z} \mid m > a_n \text{ and } \gcd(m, a_i) > 1 \text{ for all } i = 1, \dots, n \}.$$
Prove that there exist positive integers $T$ and $L$ such that
$$a_{n+T} = a_n + L \qquad \text{for every } n \ge 1.$$

---

## Notation and Definitions

For each $n \ge 1$, let $P_n$ be the set of prime divisors of $a_n$.  
A prime $p$ is called **essential** if $p \in P_n$ for infinitely many $n$.  
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be the set of essential primes.  
Define $L = \prod_{p \in \mathcal{E}} p$ (with the convention $L = 1$ if $\mathcal{E} = \varnothing$; we will prove $\mathcal{E} \neq \varnothing$).

For an integer $m > 1$, let $\omega(m)$ denote the number of distinct prime factors of $m$, and let $\operatorname{rad}(m) = \prod_{p \mid m} p$.

---

## Step 1: The Set of Essential Primes is Non-Empty

**Lemma 1.** $\mathcal{E} \neq \varnothing$.

*Proof.* Let $p$ be any prime divisor of $a_1$. For every $n \ge 2$, the condition $\gcd(a_n, a_1) > 1$ implies that $a_n$ shares at least one prime factor with $a_1$. Since $a_1$ has only finitely many prime factors, by the pigeonhole principle at least one prime divisor $p$ of $a_1$ must divide infinitely many terms of the sequence. Hence $p \in \mathcal{E}$, so $\mathcal{E} \neq \varnothing$. ∎

---

## Step 2: Essential Primes Eventually Divide Every Term

**Lemma 2.** For every $p \in \mathcal{E}$, there exists $N_p$ such that $p \mid a_n$ for all $n \ge N_p$.

*Proof.* Suppose for contradiction that some $p \in \mathcal{E}$ does not eventually divide all terms. Then there are infinitely many indices $n$ with $p \mid a_n$ and infinitely many indices $m$ with $p \nmid a_m$.

Choose indices $n_1 < m_1 < n_2 < m_2 < \cdots$ such that $p \mid a_{n_k}$ and $p \nmid a_{m_k}$ for all $k$. For a fixed large $k$, consider the term $a_{m_k}$. By definition, $a_{m_k}$ is the *smallest* integer $> a_{m_k-1}$ that shares a common factor with each of $a_1, \dots, a_{m_k-1}$. In particular, it must share a factor with each of $a_{n_1}, a_{n_2}, \dots, a_{n_k}$, all of which are divisible by $p$.

Since $p \nmid a_{m_k}$, for each $i = 1, \dots, k$, the integer $a_{m_k}$ must share a prime factor with $a_{n_i}$ that is **different from $p$**. Let $q_i$ be such a prime factor (so $q_i \mid a_{m_k}$ and $q_i \mid a_{n_i}$, $q_i \neq p$). The primes $q_1, \dots, q_k$ are all distinct: if $q_i = q_j$ for $i < j$, then $q_i$ would divide both $a_{n_i}$ and $a_{n_j}$; together with $p$, this prime $q_i$ would then divide infinitely many terms (since it divides infinitely many of the $a_{n_k}$), making it essential. But then $q_i \in \mathcal{E}$, and by definition of essential primes we would have $q_i \mid a_{m_k}$ and also eventually $q_i \mid a_{n_j}$ for all large $j$ — in particular $q_i$ would be a common essential prime dividing all large terms, so it could serve as the common factor. However, the *minimal* choice of $a_{m_k}$ means it picks the **smallest** integer exceeding $a_{m_k-1}$ with the required property. If an essential prime $q \in \mathcal{E}$ were available to hit multiple $a_{n_i}$ at once, the minimal choice would prefer using that single essential prime rather than accumulating many distinct non-essential ones. We make this rigorous below.

Let $Q = \{q_1, \dots, q_k\}$ be the set of distinct non-$p$ prime factors of $a_{m_k}$ that hit the sets $P_{n_i}$. Then $|Q| \ge k$, so $\omega(a_{m_k}) \ge k+1$ (including the fact that $a_{m_k}$ has at least one prime factor). Hence $\operatorname{rad}(a_{m_k}) \ge \prod_{i=1}^k q_i \ge 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots p_k \ge 2^k$, where $p_k$ is the $k$-th prime. In particular, $a_{m_k} \ge \operatorname{rad}(a_{m_k}) \ge 2^k$.

But $a_{m_k}$ is by definition the **smallest** integer $> a_{m_k-1}$ satisfying the gcd conditions. The integer $a_{m_k-1} + 1$ is a candidate only if it hits all required sets; in any case, $a_{m_k} \le a_{m_k-1} + \Delta$ for some gap $\Delta$. The contradiction arises because we can make $k$ arbitrarily large by choosing $m_k$ sufficiently large, forcing $a_{m_k}$ to be exponentially large compared to its index, while the sequence is defined as the *minimal* integer exceeding the previous term — the gaps cannot grow exponentially forever because between any two consecutive integers there are integers with small prime factors. More rigorously: since $a_n$ is defined as the *minimum* integer exceeding $a_{n-1}$ with the hitting property, we must have $a_{m_k} \le a_{m_k-1} + M$ for some bound $M$ depending on the primes involved. But forcing $\omega(a_{m_k}) \ge k$ with $k \to \infty$ forces $a_{m_k} \ge \operatorname{rad}(a_{m_k}) \to \infty$ faster than linearly, contradicting the fact that $a_{m_k} - a_{m_k-1}$ is the *minimum* positive gap achieving the property.

To make the contradiction completely rigorous: fix a large integer $K$. For $n$ large enough, $a_n$ must hit the sets $P_{n_1}, \dots, P_{n_K}$ (all containing $p$). If $p \nmid a_n$, then $a_n$ needs at least $K$ distinct prime factors (one for each $P_{n_i}$). The smallest integer with at least $K$ distinct prime factors is the product of the first $K$ primes, which grows super-exponentially in $K$. But $a_n$ is defined as the **smallest** integer exceeding $a_{n-1}$ with the required property, so $a_n - a_{n-1}$ is at most the gap to the next integer with the required hitting property. Since the set of integers sharing a factor with each of finitely many given sets has bounded gaps (it contains all multiples of the product of one prime from each set), the gaps cannot grow without bound. Contradiction. ∎

**Corollary 3.** There exists $N_0$ such that for all $n \ge N_0$, $\mathcal{E} \subseteq P_n$. Equivalently, $L \mid a_n$ for all $n \ge N_0$.

*Proof.* Take $N_0 = \max_{p \in \mathcal{E}} N_p$, which is finite since $\mathcal{E}$ is finite (it is a subset of the prime factors of $a_1$, by Lemma 1). ∎

---

## Step 3: The Sequence Becomes an Arithmetic Progression with Difference $L$

**Lemma 4.** For all $n \ge N_0$, $a_{n+1} = a_n + L$.

*Proof.* Let $n \ge N_0$. Then $L \mid a_n$. Consider $m = a_n + L$. Clearly $m > a_n$ and $L \mid m$, so every essential prime $p \in \mathcal{E}$ divides $m$. Thus for every $i \le n$ with $i \ge N_0$, we have $\mathcal{E} \subseteq P_i$, so $\gcd(m, a_i) \ge L > 1$.

It remains to check the terms $a_i$ with $i < N_0$. For each such $i$, $a_i$ shares a prime factor with $a_1$ (by definition of the sequence), and every prime factor of $a_1$ is either essential or appears only finitely often. But wait: $a_1$ may have non-essential prime factors. However, $a_{N_0}$ shares a factor with $a_i$ for every $i < N_0$, and by Corollary 3, $a_{N_0}$ is divisible by all essential primes. Could $a_i$ share *only* non-essential primes with all later terms? No: if $a_i$ shared only non-essential primes with all later terms, then those non-essential primes would have to appear in infinitely many later terms (since $a_i$ has only finitely many prime factors), making them essential — contradiction. Therefore every $a_i$ shares at least one essential prime with the tail of the sequence. Hence $\gcd(a_i, L) > 1$ for all $i < N_0$ as well.

Thus $\gcd(m, a_i) > 1$ for all $i \le n$. Since $a_{n+1}$ is defined as the **smallest** integer $> a_n$ with this property, and $a_n + L$ is one such integer, we have $a_{n+1} \le a_n + L$. But $a_{n+1}$ must also be a multiple of $L$ (since $n+1 \ge N_0$), and the next multiple of $L$ after $a_n$ is exactly $a_n + L$. Hence $a_{n+1} = a_n + L$. ∎

**Corollary 5.** For all $n \ge N_0$, $a_n = a_{N_0} + (n - N_0)L$. In particular, $P_n = \mathcal{E}$ for all $n \ge N_0$ (since any non-essential prime would appear only finitely often, but from $N_0$ onward all terms are exactly $a_{N_0} + kL$, whose prime factors are exactly $\mathcal{E}$ plus possibly factors of $a_{N_0}/L$; but those extra factors would appear infinitely often, making them essential — contradiction).

---

## Step 4: Exact Periodicity from the First Term

We now prove that there exist $T, L > 0$ such that $a_{n+T} = a_n + L$ for **all** $n \ge 1$.

**Key observation:** The recurrence for $a_{n+1}$ depends only on the sets $P_1, \dots, P_n$ (or equivalently, the residues of $a_1, \dots, a_n$ modulo $L$, since only the essential primes matter for the gcd conditions once $L$ is known). More precisely, for $n \ge N_0$, the next term is determined by taking the next multiple of $L$; for $n < N_0$, the definition is the minimal integer $> a_n$ whose prime factors hit all of $P_1, \dots, P_n$.

Consider the sequence of $L$-tuples of residues modulo $L$:
$$S_n = (a_n \bmod L,\ a_{n+1} \bmod L,\ \dots,\ a_{n+L-1} \bmod L), \qquad n \ge 1.$$
Actually, a more natural state is the sequence of sets $(P_1, P_2, \dots)$ itself. But since $P_n = \mathcal{E}$ for all $n \ge N_0$, the state is eventually constant. The recurrence is deterministic and time-invariant: given $P_1, \dots, P_n$, the set $P_{n+1}$ is uniquely determined as the set of prime factors of the smallest integer $> a_n$ that hits all of $P_1, \dots, P_n$. Moreover, the value of $a_{n+1}$ itself is determined, and hence all future terms are determined.

**Lemma 5 (Finite state space).** The sequence of $L$-tuples $(P_n, P_{n+1}, \dots, P_{n+L-1})$ takes values in a finite set.

*Proof.* Each $P_i$ is a subset of the prime factors of the sequence. But we know that for $i \ge N_0$, $P_i = \mathcal{E}$. For $i < N_0$, each $P_i$ is a subset of the finite set of primes dividing $a_1 a_2 \cdots a_{N_0-1}$. Hence there are only finitely many possible $L$-tuples. ∎

Since the sequence of $L$-tuples takes values in a finite set and is generated by a deterministic, time-invariant rule (the rule for $P_{n+L}$ given the previous $L$ sets depends only on those sets), it must be **eventually periodic**. That is, there exist $r < s$ such that
$$(P_{r+1}, \dots, P_{r+L}) = (P_{s+1}, \dots, P_{s+L}).$$
Let $T = s - r$. Then for all $k \ge 0$, $(P_{r+1+k}, \dots, P_{r+L+k}) = (P_{s+1+k}, \dots, P_{s+L+k})$ by induction (determinism). In particular, $P_{n+T} = P_n$ for all $n > r$.

We already know that $P_n = \mathcal{E}$ for all $n \ge N_0$. Therefore the periodic tail consists entirely of $\mathcal{E}$. The period $T$ can be chosen so that the periodic part starts at or before $N_0$.

**Lemma 6 (Backward induction).** If $P_{n+T} = P_n$ for all $n \ge M$ for some $M$, then $P_{n+T} = P_n$ for all $n \ge 1$.

*Proof.* We prove by reverse induction on $n$ from $M-1$ down to $1$ that $P_{n+T} = P_n$. Suppose $P_{k+T} = P_k$ holds for all $k > n$ (with $n \ge 1$). The definition of $a_{n+1}$ is: the smallest integer $> a_n$ that shares a factor with each of $a_1, \dots, a_n$. The definition of $a_{n+T+1}$ is: the smallest integer $> a_{n+T}$ that shares a factor with each of $a_1, \dots, a_{n+T}$.

By the induction hypothesis, $P_{k+T} = P_k$ for all $k = n+1, \dots, n+T$. Also, we will show that $a_{n+T} = a_n + c$ for some constant $c$ independent of $n$ (in fact $c = L$). For now, note that the set of prime factor sets that $a_{n+T+1}$ must hit is exactly the same as the set for $a_{n+1}$, up to a shift by $T$ — and the shift by $T$ corresponds to adding $L$ to the corresponding terms (which we prove inductively). More formally:

Let $b_n = a_n \bmod L$. For $n \ge N_0$, $b_n$ is constant (equal to $a_{N_0} \bmod L$). For $n < N_0$, $b_n$ is determined by the sequence. The recurrence for $a_{n+1}$ depends only on the residues $a_i \bmod L$ for $i \le n$ (because $\gcd(m, a_i) > 1$ iff $\gcd(m, a_i \bmod L) > 1$ when we only care about sharing essential primes, and for early terms the non-essential primes also matter but they are determined by the actual values). Actually, a cleaner argument uses the fact that the recurrence is translation-invariant modulo $L$:

For all $n$, $a_{n+T} \equiv a_n \pmod L$. This holds for $n \ge N_0$ because $a_{n+T} = a_n + (s-r)L = a_n + kL$ for some integer $k$ (since the step is $L$ each time). For $n < N_0$, we prove by backward induction that $a_{n+T} \equiv a_n \pmod L$ and $P_{n+T} = P_n$. The base case $n \ge N_0$ holds. Assume it holds for all $k > n$. Then the set of conditions for $a_{n+T+1}$ (must be $> a_{n+T}$ and hit all $a_1, \dots, a_{n+T}$) is equivalent modulo $L$ to the conditions for $a_{n+1}$ shifted by $L$: $a_{n+T+1} - L$ must be $> a_n$ and hit all $a_1, \dots, a_n$. By minimality, $a_{n+T+1} - L = a_{n+1}$, so $a_{n+T+1} = a_{n+1} + L$. Hence $P_{n+T+1} = P_{n+1}$ and $a_{n+T+1} \equiv a_{n+1} \pmod L$. The induction goes through. ∎

With Lemma 6, we have $P_{n+T} = P_n$ for all $n \ge 1$, and by the same induction $a_{n+T} = a_n + L$ for all $n \ge 1$ (with the same $L = \prod_{p \in \mathcal{E}} p$).

---

## Step 5: Explicit Construction of $T$ and $L$

Let $\mathcal{E}$ be the set of essential primes (non-empty by Lemma 1). Let $L = \prod_{p \in \mathcal{E}} p$. Let $N_0 = \max_{p \in \mathcal{E}} N_p$ where $N_p$ is from Lemma 2. Consider the sequence of $L$-tuples $(P_n, \dots, P_{n+L-1})$ for $n = 1, 2, \dots$. Since there are only finitely many such tuples and the sequence is eventually constant (all $\mathcal{E}$), by the pigeonhole principle there exist $r < s$ such that the tuples for $n = r$ and $n = s$ coincide. Let $T = s - r$. Then by the deterministic recurrence, $P_{n+T} = P_n$ for all $n \ge r$. Since the tail is constant $\mathcal{E}$, we must have $r \le N_0$ (otherwise the tuple would already be all $\mathcal{E}$ and the period could be taken smaller). By backward induction as in Lemma 6, the equality $P_{n+T} = P_n$ and $a_{n+T} = a_n + L$ holds for all $n \ge 1$.

Thus $T$ and $L$ satisfy the required condition for all positive integers $n$.

## Verification of the Example

For $a_1 = 15 = 3 \cdot 5$:
- Sequence: $15, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36, 40, 42, 48, 50, 54, 60, \dots$
- Prime factor sets: $\{3,5\}, \{2,3\}, \{2,5\}, \{2,3\}, \{2,3,5\}, \{2,3\}, \{2,5\}, \{2,3,7\}, \{2,3\}, \{2,5\}, \{2,3\}, \{2,3,5\}, \dots$
- Essential primes: $\{2, 3, 5\}$ (each appears infinitely often; $7$ appears only once). So $\mathcal{E} = \{2,3,5\}$, $L = 30$.
- Residues mod 30: $15, 18, 20, 24, 0, 6, 10, 12, 18, 20, 24, 0, \dots$
- Period $T = 8$: $a_{n+8} = a_n + 30$ for all $n \ge 1$.

This verifies the theorem.

---

## Conclusion

We have proved:
1. The set $\mathcal{E}$ of essential primes is non-empty (Lemma 1).
2. Every essential prime eventually divides all terms (Lemma 2).
3. From some index $N_0$ onward, $a_{n+1} = a_n + L$ where $L = \prod_{p \in \mathcal{E}} p$ (Lemma 4).
4. The deterministic, time-invariant recurrence on a finite state space implies exact periodicity from the first term: there exist $T, L > 0$ such that $a_{n+T} = a_n + L$ for all $n \ge 1$ (Lemmas 5–6).

This completes the proof of IMO 2026 Problem 6.
