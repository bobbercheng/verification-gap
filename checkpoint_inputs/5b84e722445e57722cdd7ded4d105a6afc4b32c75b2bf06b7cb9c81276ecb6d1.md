## Problem
Let $ABC$ be a triangle and let points $M$ and $N$ be the midpoints of sides $AB$ and $AC$, respectively. Let points $K$ and $L$ be chosen inside triangles $BMC$ and $BNC$, respectively, such that $K$ lies inside the angle $LBA$, $L$ lies inside the angle $ACK$, and $\angle KBA = \angle ACL$, $\angle LBK = \angle LNC$, $\angle LCK = \angle BMK$. Let $O$ be the circumcentre of triangle $AKL$. Prove that $OM = ON$.

## Approaches tried
- **Power/circle-equation reformulation:** $OM=ON$ is equivalent to equal powers of $M,N$ with respect to the circumcircle of $AKL$. In an oblique coordinate system along $AB,AC$, this becomes one linear identity in the two coefficients of the circle equation.
- **Angle parametrization and sine law (active):** put $\alpha=\angle A$, $\beta=\angle B$, $\gamma=\angle C$ and $x=\angle KBA=\angle ACL$, $y=\angle LBK=\angle LNC$, $z=\angle LCK=\angle BMK$. Interior conditions force the ray orders $BA,BK,BL,BC$ at $B$ and $CA,CL,CK,CB$ at $C$. Sine-law formulas have been obtained for $BK$ and $CL$, yielding separate quadratic trigonometric equations for $y,z$.
- **Symbolic factorization (active):** the prepared SymPy script now runs via `uv run --offline --with sympy`. The raw target expression has been computed, but no useful factorization has yet appeared because the two sine-law constraints have not been substituted in a suitable algebraic parametrization.
- **Numerical exploration:** several non-isosceles test triangles and admissible angle choices were solved numerically; each of the separate equations for $y,z$ appears to have exactly one admissible root. No simple relation such as $y=z$ or $bBK=cCL$ holds, so the proof must use the full constraints rather than a naive symmetry relation.

## Current best
Let $AB=c$, $AC=b$, $BC=a$, and write $r=BK$, $d=CL$. The established formulas are
\[
r=\frac c2\frac{\sin z}{\sin(x+z)}
=a\frac{\sin(\gamma-x-z)}{\sin(\alpha+2x+z)},
\qquad
d=\frac b2\frac{\sin y}{\sin(x+y)}
=a\frac{\sin(\beta-x-y)}{\sin(\alpha+2x+y)}. \tag{1}
\]
Using unit vectors along $AB,AC$, the coordinates of $K,L$ are
\[
K=\left(c-r\frac{\sin(\alpha+x)}{\sin\alpha},
 r\frac{\sin x}{\sin\alpha}\right),\quad
L=\left(d\frac{\sin x}{\sin\alpha},
 b-d\frac{\sin(\alpha+x)}{\sin\alpha}\right). \tag{2}
\]
A circle through $A$ has equation $s^2+t^2+2st\cos\alpha-ps-qt=0$; its centre satisfies $2O\cdot\mathbf e_{AB}=p$, $2O\cdot\mathbf e_{AC}=q$. Hence the desired result is exactly
\[
2bq-2cp=b^2-c^2. \tag{3}
\]
Numerical work confirms the angle equations and indicates that an exact elimination/factorization is plausible. The open gap is deriving (3) cleanly from (1), either by a trigonometric lemma valid separately for the $B$- and $C$-side constructions or by factoring the circle determinant after rational parametrization.

## Full proof
A complete proof has not yet been obtained. The rigorous reduction currently established is as follows.

Orient the triangle so that the interior rays have orders $BA,BK,BL,BC$ at $B$ and $CA,CL,CK,CB$ at $C$. Define $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,x,y,z$ as above. In triangle $BMK$, since $BM=c/2$, $\angle MBK=x$, and $\angle BMK=z$, the sine law gives
\[
BK=\frac c2\frac{\sin z}{\sin(x+z)}.
\]
At $B$, $\angle KBC=\beta-x$ (the intermediate ray $BL$ splits this as $y+(\beta-x-y)$). At $C$, $\angle BCK=\gamma-x-z$, because $\angle LCK=z$ and $\angle ACL=x$. Therefore the third angle of $BCK$ is $\alpha+2x+z$, and the sine law in $BCK$ gives
\[
BK=a\frac{\sin(\gamma-x-z)}{\sin(\alpha+2x+z)}.
\]
Likewise, triangle $CNL$ has $CN=b/2$, $\angle NCL=x$, and $\angle CNL=y$, whence
\[
CL=\frac b2\frac{\sin y}{\sin(x+y)}.
\]
At $B$, $\angle LBC=\beta-x-y$, and at $C$, $\angle BCL=\gamma-x$; hence the third angle of $BCL$ is $\alpha+2x+y$, and its sine law gives the other formula for $CL$ in (1). (Here $\angle BLC=\alpha+2x+y$ follows from the angle sum.)

Coordinates (2) follow by resolving a segment making angle $x$ with $BA$ or $CA$ in the oblique basis. Substitution of $K,L$ into the circle equation determines $p,q$, and direct expansion shows that (3) is the sole remaining algebraic assertion. Its proof from (1) remains open.
